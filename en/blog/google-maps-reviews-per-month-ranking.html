How Many Google Maps Reviews Per Month Do You Need to Keep Your Ranking?

There is no universal magic number. The reviews you need per month depend on your zone: aim to match the monthly review pace of the #1 business in your category and area, plus about 20%. For most urban restaurants that lands between 15 and 40 fresh reviews a month, but a dentist or boutique in the same city may only need 4 to 10. What matters is relative velocity — your pace versus your top competitor — and recency, because fresh reviews weigh more than an old pile of ratings.

By Pulse · 2026-11-07 · 5 min read

Every local business owner eventually asks the same question: how many reviews do I need each month to hold my spot on Google Maps? The honest answer isn't a fixed number — it's relative to your zone. A café in downtown Miami competing against rivals pulling 50 reviews a month plays a completely different game than a hardware store in a quieter district. The right target is defined by your competition, not by a blog promising "get 10 reviews and you'll rank."

What "review velocity" actually means

Review velocity is the rate at which new reviews arrive over time — reviews per week or per month — not your total lifetime count. Google's local ranking leans on three pillars: relevance, distance, and prominence. Reviews feed prominence, but a steady flow signals something a static total never will: that your business is active and currently relevant. A profile with 800 reviews that stopped growing two years ago looks stale next to one adding 20 fresh ones a month.

This is why two businesses with the same total can rank very differently. The one accumulating reviews now sends a stronger, more current signal than the one living off its old reputation.

The formula: your competitor's pace +20%

Forget the universal number. Use this instead:

  1. Identify the #1 business in your exact category and zone (the top result in your local pack for your main keyword).
  2. Count their new reviews over the last 90 days and divide by 3 to get their monthly pace.
  3. Add 20%. That's your monthly target.

Example: the top-ranked pizzeria in your area gained 45 reviews in the last 90 days — that's 15 per month. Your target becomes 18 per month. Beat the leader's pace consistently and you close the prominence gap in the signal that's easiest to move.

This approach works because Google ranks you against your actual neighbors, not against a national average. Matching an abstract benchmark is useless if the guy two blocks over is outpacing it.

Reference table by business type

These are realistic monthly ranges for competitive zones in major U.S. and LATAM cities. Treat them as starting points — always re-anchor to your real #1 competitor.

  • Busy urban restaurant: 20–40 reviews/month to stay in the top pack.
  • Café / fast-casual: 15–30/month.
  • Retail / boutique: 8–20/month.
  • Dental / medical clinic: 4–12/month.
  • Professional / local service (legal, accounting, repair): 3–10/month.
  • Gym / fitness studio: 10–25/month.

A well-positioned urban restaurant typically carries several hundred to a few thousand total reviews — but it got there and stays there by maintaining pace, not by hitting a one-time milestone.

The recency rule: fresh beats accumulated

Recent reviews carry more weight than old ones. Google and your potential customers both read recency as proof the experience is good right now. A 4.6 built last month reassures more than a 4.8 whose last review is from 2022.

Two practical consequences:

  • If you stop receiving reviews for a few weeks, your rating won't drop overnight — but your freshness signal decays and competitors who keep the flow going start to pull ahead in the pack.
  • Replying to reviews matters too. Responses are an activity signal, and they turn a static review into an ongoing conversation Google can see.

Common mistakes that waste your effort

  • Chasing a universal number. "10 a month" is meaningless if your competitor does 30.
  • Buying bursts of reviews. A sudden spike of unnatural reviews is the fastest way to get filtered or penalized. Steady and real beats fast and fake.
  • Counting total instead of velocity. Vanity in the total number; the trend line is what moves ranking.
  • Ignoring responses. Collecting reviews but never replying leaves half the signal on the table.
  • Setting one target for a whole chain. Each location competes in its own zone with its own leader.

Multi-location: one target per branch, not per brand

If you run several locations, a single company-wide goal is a trap. Your downtown branch might face a leader doing 40/month while your suburban one competes against a leader doing 12. Set the target per location:

  • Find the #1 competitor for each branch's specific zone.
  • Apply the +20% formula to each independently.
  • Give each store manager their number — not a shared brand KPI.
  • Review the pace weekly, because your competitors' pace shifts too.

A new branch starting from zero is a special case: you won't match an established leader in month one. Aim first for the leader's monthly pace (not their total), build momentum, and expect three to six months of consistent flow before you're genuinely competing for the pack.

Frequently asked questions

Does total review count or review frequency matter more?

Frequency matters more for staying competitive. A steady stream of fresh reviews signals your business is active and currently relevant, while a large but stagnant total looks stale. Total count builds credibility, but velocity is what keeps you moving in the local pack.

Does Google penalize you for getting many reviews at once?

A sudden, unnatural spike of reviews — especially bought or fake ones — can trigger Google's filters and get them removed or flagged. Organic bursts from a real event are fine. The safest approach is a consistent, authentic pace rather than one big dump.

How many reviews does the #1 business in my category and zone have?

It varies by city and category, but the relevant number isn't their total — it's their pace. Count the reviews they gained in the last 90 days, divide by 3 for a monthly rate, and make your target that number plus 20%. Re-check monthly since competitors' pace changes.

How much more do recent reviews count versus old ones?

Recent reviews carry noticeably more weight because both Google and customers read them as proof of current quality. A rating built from fresh reviews reassures more than a slightly higher score whose last review is years old. Keeping a steady flow protects your freshness signal.

How many reviews do I need for a brand-new branch starting from zero?

You won't match an established leader's total in month one, so don't try. Aim for the leader's monthly pace instead, build momentum, and expect three to six months of consistent flow before you genuinely compete for the local pack in that zone.

What happens to my rating if I stop getting reviews for a few weeks?

Your star rating won't drop immediately, but your freshness signal decays and active competitors start pulling ahead in the pack. A few quiet weeks won't sink you, but a prolonged gap lets rivals who keep the flow going overtake your position.
