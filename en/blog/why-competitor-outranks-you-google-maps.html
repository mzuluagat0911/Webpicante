Why Your Competitor Outranks You on Google Maps (and How to Win Back the Customers Google Is Sending Them)

Your competitor outranks you on Google Maps because Google scores every business on three axes—relevance, distance and prominence—and they simply score higher on the mix that matters for a given search. Prominence (rating, review volume, review freshness, replies and activity) is the axis you can actually move, so the fastest way to win back customers is to audit the competitor beating you and close the specific gap.

By Pulse · 2026-07-26 · 5 min read

If people in your area search for what you sell and the business next door shows up first, it isn't bad luck. Google Maps is ranking two real businesses against each other, and right now your competitor is winning the comparison. About 90% of consumers check Google before choosing a local business, so every position you lose on Maps is a customer walking into someone else's door. The good news: the ranking is measurable, and the gap is closeable once you know exactly where you're behind.

The 3 axes of the Google Maps algorithm, seen as a head-to-head

Google ranks local results on three factors. The mistake most owners make is reading them as a solo checklist. Read them instead as "why the business next door beats me."

1. Relevance — do you match the search better than they do?

Relevance is how well your profile matches what the person typed. If a competitor has the exact category, well-described services, and keywords your customers actually use, Google reads them as a closer match. If your primary category is generic ("Restaurant" vs. their "Sushi Restaurant"), they win before distance even matters.

2. Distance — how close are you to the searcher?

Distance is the proximity between the searcher and each business. This is the axis you can't change—but it's also the one owners over-blame. Google will surface a business slightly farther away if it's far more prominent. So "we're in the same zone" is exactly why the other two axes decide the winner.

3. Prominence — how established and active do you look?

Prominence is how well-known and trusted your business appears. It's built from rating, number of reviews, review recency, your replies to reviews, photos, and overall activity. This is the battleground. When two businesses tie on relevance and are equally close, prominence breaks the tie—and it's almost entirely within your control.

The reframe: You don't need to be perfect on Google Maps. You need to out-score the specific competitor taking your customers. That turns an impossible checklist into a winnable, comparative game.

How to audit the competitor beating you in 20 minutes

Before you change anything, find out why they rank better. Open the search a customer would type and study the business above you.

  1. Compare categories. Note their primary and secondary categories vs. yours. A mismatch here is the most common invisible reason a competitor ranks first.
  2. Count reviews and rating. Write down their total reviews and star average next to yours. Volume and rating together drive prominence.
  3. Check review freshness. When was their last review? A steady flow of recent reviews signals an active, alive business. Ten reviews this month beats 200 from three years ago.
  4. Read their replies. Do they respond to reviews? How fast, and in what tone? Active replies signal engagement to both Google and customers.
  5. Read what customers praise and complain about. Their reviews are free market research. If people repeatedly praise "fast service" or complain about "parking," you've found where to differentiate.
  6. Look at photos and posts. Fresh, frequent photos and Google posts are activity signals. Stale profiles lose ground over time.

By the end you'll have a two-column scorecard: you vs. them, axis by axis. The gaps are your to-do list.

How to close the gap and win back customers

Prioritize actions by impact, not effort. Here's the order that moves the needle fastest.

  • Fix relevance first (one afternoon). Set the most specific primary category, fill every service and attribute, and describe your business with the words customers search. This is free and immediate.
  • Systematize review generation. If a competitor has more reviews, close that gap with a repeatable ask—QR at checkout, follow-up message, a link staff share. Consistency beats bursts.
  • Reply to every review, fast. Replies signal activity and improve perceived trust. Answer positives briefly and negatives with a real solution—future customers read both.
  • Protect your rating. A handful of unanswered 1-stars can sink you below a competitor. Address the recurring complaints their reviews (and yours) reveal.
  • Stay visibly active. Add photos and posts regularly. A profile that's updated weekly outranks one frozen in time.

Common mistakes that keep you behind

Owners lose the Maps race for predictable reasons:

  • Blaming distance for everything. If a farther competitor outranks you, the problem is relevance or prominence—both fixable.
  • Chasing a magic review number. There's no fixed threshold; you need to beat your competitor's volume, rating and recency, not hit a universal count.
  • Ignoring review replies. Silence reads as inactivity to Google and as neglect to customers.
  • Auditing once and stopping. Competitors add reviews, change categories and open new locations. A one-time snapshot goes stale in weeks, which is why rankings drop "suddenly" month to month.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my competitor rank above me on Google Maps if we're in the same area?

When you're both equally close to the searcher, distance stops being the deciding factor and relevance and prominence break the tie. Your competitor likely has a more specific category, more or fresher reviews, a higher rating, or more activity—so Google reads them as the better, more trusted match.

How much do reviews weigh versus distance and relevance in Maps rankings?

Reviews are part of prominence, one of Google's three ranking axes alongside relevance and distance. You can't change your location, so among the factors you control, reviews—volume, rating, recency and your replies—carry the most weight for closing the gap with a nearby competitor.

How many reviews do I need to outrank a competitor?

There's no universal number. The goal is to beat that specific competitor's review volume, rating and freshness, not hit a fixed count. A steady flow of recent, high-rated reviews with replies usually outperforms a larger pile of old ones.

Does replying to reviews help me rank higher on Google Maps?

Yes. Replying signals activity and engagement to Google and builds trust with future customers who read your responses. Answering every review quickly—especially negatives with a real solution—strengthens the prominence side of your ranking.

How can I see my competitors' ratings and reviews?

You can check each competitor's profile manually to note their rating, review count, recency and replies. To track this continuously across multiple competitors and locations, tools like Pulse rank your zone live and alert you when a rival's reputation shifts.
