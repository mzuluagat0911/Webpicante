How Often Should You Update Your Google Business Profile? The Real Routine to Stay on the Map

Update your Google Business Profile on a mixed cadence: respond to reviews and messages daily, add photos and a post weekly, and audit hours, categories and services monthly (plus every holiday). Google doesn't reward a single big update — it rewards recent, consistent activity. In dense cities, a profile that goes quiet for weeks loses relevance to competitors who keep feeding theirs, which can cost you a spot in the local top 3.

By Pulse · 2026-09-05 · 5 min read

"Keep your profile updated" is useless advice. Update it how often — and with what? The honest answer is that Google Business Profile (GBP) isn't one task, it's a routine with different cadences. Some actions move ranking (activity and prominence). Others don't move ranking at all but decide whether a searcher who found you actually walks in. Confusing the two is why most local businesses either overwork the wrong things or let their profile go stale. Below is the real calendar — daily, weekly, monthly — with time estimates and a multi-location view.

Why frequency matters: activity is a ranking signal

Google's local ranking rests on three factors: relevance (does your profile match the search), distance (how close you are), and prominence (how known and trusted you are). Prominence is where update frequency lives. A profile that gets fresh reviews, replies, photos and posts sends a steady signal that the business is open, active and cared for. A profile that hasn't moved in two months sends the opposite.

Does Google demote inactive profiles outright? Not with a formal penalty flag. What happens is quieter and more dangerous: your competitors keep accumulating recent signals while you don't. In a big city with dozens of options per category, relative activity is everything. Standing still isn't neutral — it's ceding ground. Around 90% of people check Google before choosing a local business, so the profile that looks alive wins the click.

The routine, by cadence (with time estimates)

Here's the split that actually keeps a single location competitive without turning GBP into a full-time job.

Daily (5–10 minutes)

  • Reply to new reviews — every one, positive and negative. Response speed and consistency read as activity. Aim to reply within 24–48 hours.
  • Check messages and Q&A — unanswered questions sit there publicly and kill conversion.
  • Scan for edits — Google or users can suggest changes to your hours or address; catch wrong edits before they spread.

Weekly (20–30 minutes)

  • Add 2–3 new photos — real, recent shots: dishes, the space, staff, a busy night. Fresh photos are one of the easiest recurring activity signals.
  • Publish one post/update — an offer, event, new item or news. Posts keep the profile fresh and give repeat context to Google.
  • Review the week's ratings trend — a sudden dip means an operational problem worth fixing now, not next month.

Monthly (30–45 minutes)

  • Audit hours for the coming weeks and set special hours for holidays in advance.
  • Check categories, services and attributes — the primary category is one of the strongest relevance levers; make sure it's still the most accurate one.
  • Refresh the description and menu/services if anything changed.
  • Verify NAP consistency (name, address, phone) against your website and other listings.

Ranking signals vs. conversion hygiene — don't confuse them

This is the distinction most guides skip. Some updates build prominence (they help you rank); others are conversion hygiene (they don't move ranking but decide whether the searcher chooses you).

  • Moves ranking (activity + prominence): new reviews, review replies, fresh photos, regular posts, consistent engagement over time.
  • Conversion hygiene: correct hours, accurate phone, clear menu/prices, good primary photo, complete services. A wrong hour won't tank your rank — but it sends a ready customer to the place next door.

Is prominence the same as recent activity? No. Prominence is the accumulated trust (total reviews, overall rating, mentions, links), while recent activity is the steady stream of new signals. You need both: prominence is your foundation, recent activity is what keeps that foundation from decaying while rivals build theirs.

Common mistakes that quietly cost you the top 3

  • The "set it and forget it" profile. You filled everything out once in 2022. It looks complete — and completely inactive.
  • Batching a month of posts then going silent. Consistency beats bursts. One post a week beats ten posts then nothing for six weeks.
  • Ignoring holiday hours. "Open" when you're closed generates bad experiences and, over time, edits and complaints. Setting special hours before each holiday protects both trust and visibility.
  • Replying only to bad reviews. Replying to positive ones too doubles your activity signal and models tone for future customers.
  • Stock or duplicated photos. Recycled images add nothing; recent, real photos are the point.

Multi-location: one standard, many zones

The routine above is manageable for one location. At 5, 10 or 30 branches it becomes a coordination problem — and the failure mode is predictable: two locations shine while the rest drift. The fix is roles and rhythm, not heroics.

  • Owner/ops director: sets the standard and reviews a weekly rollup — which branches are behind on reviews, photos or replies.
  • Local manager: owns the daily 5–10 minutes at their branch — replies, messages, a photo or two. They're closest to reality and best placed to feed the profile.
  • Marketing/ops: handles monthly audits (categories, hours, holidays) across all locations from one place.

The goal is one criterion applied everywhere, so no branch quietly falls off the map while HQ assumes it's fine.

The bottom line

There's no single "update it every X days" number — there's a rhythm. Daily reviews and messages, weekly photos and a post, monthly hours-and-categories audit, plus holiday hours set in advance. The businesses that hold the top 3 in dense cities aren't doing anything magical; they're just never letting the profile go quiet while everyone around them keeps moving.

Frequently asked questions

Does Google lower the ranking of inactive profiles?

There's no formal penalty flag, but inactive profiles lose ground in practice. Ranking depends on prominence, which is fed by recent activity — reviews, replies, photos, posts. If your competitors keep generating those signals and you don't, you fall behind them, especially in dense cities with many options per category.

How often should I add new photos?

Add 2–3 fresh photos every week. Use real, recent shots of your space, products, staff or busy moments — not stock or recycled images. Regular new photos are one of the easiest recurring activity signals and also help searchers choose you.

Do GBP posts/updates help ranking or are they just visible?

Posts contribute to activity and freshness, which support prominence, and they also give Google recurring context about your business. They won't single-handedly rank you, but publishing one post a week consistently helps more than posting in bursts. They also drive conversion by showing offers, events and news to searchers.

How often should I reply to reviews for it to count as activity?

Reply to every new review within 24–48 hours, positive and negative. It's the response consistency and speed that read as activity, not a set weekly quota. Replying to positive reviews too doubles your signal and shows tone to future customers.

What must I update weekly vs. monthly?

Weekly: add 2–3 photos, publish one post, reply to reviews and messages, and check your ratings trend. Monthly: audit hours (including upcoming holidays), verify categories, services and attributes, refresh your description, and check name-address-phone consistency across listings.

How do I keep 5, 10 or 30 locations active without going crazy?

Split roles and rhythm: local managers handle the daily 5–10 minutes at their branch, ops runs monthly audits across all locations, and the owner reviews a weekly rollup of which branches are behind. Apply one standard everywhere so no location quietly falls off the map, and use a monitoring tool to flag the ones that need attention.
