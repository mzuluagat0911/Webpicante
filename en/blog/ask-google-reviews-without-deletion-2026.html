How to Ask for Google Reviews Without Getting Them Deleted (The 2026 Rule Change)

You can legally ask every customer for a Google review, but under Google's 2026 policy you cannot gate them — meaning you can't route happy customers to Google while diverting unhappy ones to a private form. That selective filtering is banned, and Google now actively removes gated reviews and can restrict your profile. The safe method: ask everyone the same way, with a direct link, no incentives, and no screening.

By Pulse · 2026-08-08 · 5 min read

Getting more Google reviews is one of the fastest ways to win your local zone in Maps — but only if the method survives Google's rules. The 2026 policy update ended the gray area around review gating and, more importantly, turned on active enforcement. That means a review-collection tactic that felt clever last year can now get your reviews wiped or your Business Profile restricted. For a single restaurant that's annoying. Across ten locations, it's a reputation problem that spreads fast.

What changed in Google's 2026 review policy

Review gating is the practice of screening customers before sending them to Google: happy customers get the review link, unhappy ones get routed to a private feedback form so their complaint never becomes a public star rating. Google has technically discouraged this for years — the 2026 change is that enforcement is now automated and consistent.

In practice, three things are true under the current policy:

  • You are allowed to ask for reviews. Asking every customer is fully legitimate and encouraged.
  • You cannot filter by sentiment. Any funnel that treats happy and unhappy customers differently before they reach Google is a violation.
  • Enforcement is active. Google can delete reviews collected through gating and can flag or restrict the profile behind them.

The reasoning is simple: gated ratings are artificially inflated, so they stop being a trustworthy signal. Google's ranking leans on rating, review volume, responses and activity — and it protects those signals from manipulation.

This is allowed / This is not

Use this as your operating table. If a tactic lands in the right column, retire it.

Allowed

  • Asking every customer, in person or by message, to leave a Google review.
  • Sending a direct review link or QR code to your Google profile.
  • Reminding customers once, politely, if they said they'd leave a review.
  • Responding to every review — positive and negative — in your brand voice.
  • Making it easy: table cards, receipt lines, a link after service.

Not allowed

  • Asking "How was your experience?" first and only sending happy customers to Google.
  • Routing unhappy customers to a private form instead of Google.
  • Offering discounts, free items, or entries in a raffle in exchange for a review.
  • Buying reviews or generating them from people who never visited.
  • Bulk-blasting review requests to contacts who aren't real customers.

A compliant request script (table, ticket, WhatsApp, QR)

The safest script does two things: it asks everyone, and it never screens by mood. Here's the same ask adapted to each touchpoint.

At the table / counter

"If you enjoyed today, a Google review really helps us — here's the QR, it takes 30 seconds." You can still hand a private feedback card too, but give it to everyone, not just the ones who look unhappy.

On the ticket or receipt

Print a short line with a QR: "Tell us how we did on Google → [QR]." No sentiment question, no branching.

By WhatsApp or SMS

"Thanks for visiting [Location]! If you have 30 seconds, we'd love your honest review on Google: [direct link]." The word honest matters — you're inviting a review, not steering the rating.

QR placement

Put the QR where the experience ends: the table, the exit, the bag insert. One tap should open the review box, not a survey that decides where the customer goes next.

The 20-minute weekly review ritual

Reviews don't come from a one-time push; they come from a repeatable habit that's identical across locations. Run this once a week, per location, with the same owner:

  1. Minutes 0–5: Check new reviews across every location. Note the rating trend, not just the count.
  2. Minutes 5–15: Respond to every new review in brand voice — thank the positives, address the negatives calmly and factually.
  3. Minutes 15–20: Confirm the team asked for reviews the compliant way this week (QR stocked, link working, no gating creeping back in).

The goal is a steady, even flow of real reviews across all your zones — not a spike at one branch that looks unnatural to Google.

Common mistakes that get reviews deleted

  • The "1 to 5" pre-screen. Any tool that asks for a rating first and only forwards 4–5 stars to Google is textbook gating.
  • Incentives. "Leave a review, get 10% off" violates policy even if the review is genuine.
  • Uneven bursts. Twenty reviews in one day at one location, none at the others, reads as manipulation.
  • Ignoring negatives. Not responding doesn't get you penalized, but it kills the reputation signal reviews were supposed to build.
  • Trusting a legacy tool. Many review platforms were built around gating funnels. If yours still branches by sentiment, it's a liability now.

Audit your current review tool (checklist)

Before you send another request, run your tool through this:

  • Does it ask for a rating before sending someone to Google? If yes — it gates.
  • Does it route low ratings to a private form instead of Google? If yes — it gates.
  • Does it offer or mention any incentive? Remove it.
  • Does it send the same ask to every customer regardless of sentiment? That's what you want.
  • Can it show review volume and rating trend per location, side by side?
  • Does it help you respond in your own voice, not canned templates?

The bottom line

Asking for reviews is safe, legal and necessary. Gating them — filtering by sentiment, incentivizing, or blasting fake ones — is what gets them deleted and your profile restricted under the 2026 rules. Ask everyone the same way, respond to everything, keep the flow even across locations, and audit any tool that still branches by rating. That's how you grow reviews that Google keeps.

Frequently asked questions

What is review gating and why is it banned?

Review gating is screening customers by sentiment before sending them to Google — happy ones get the review link, unhappy ones get diverted to a private form. Google bans it because it artificially inflates ratings and makes them an unreliable signal. Under the 2026 policy, gated reviews can be deleted and the profile can be restricted.

Can I ask my customers for reviews, or will Google penalize me?

You can ask every customer for a Google review — that's fully allowed and encouraged. The penalty only comes from how you ask: filtering by mood, offering incentives, or generating fake reviews. Ask everyone the same way with a direct link and you're safe.

Is it allowed to send unhappy customers to a private feedback form?

You can offer a private feedback channel, but only if you offer it to everyone equally and never use it to keep negative experiences off Google. Routing unhappy customers to a form instead of Google is gating, and it's a policy violation that can trigger deletions.

Can Google delete my reviews or restrict my profile for asking the wrong way?

Yes. Under active 2026 enforcement, Google can remove reviews collected through gating or incentives and can flag or restrict the Business Profile behind them. Legitimate, evenly requested reviews from real customers are not at risk.

Can I offer a discount in exchange for a review?

No. Any incentive — discounts, free items, raffle entries — for leaving a review violates Google's policy, even if the review itself is honest. Ask for the review on its own, with no reward attached.

How do I ask for reviews across multiple locations without breaking the rules?

Use one identical, non-gating method at every branch and keep the flow steady rather than spiking at one location. Monitor rating and volume per location so no single branch drifts or looks manipulated. Tools like Pulse let you track and respond across all zones with one consistent standard.
