How Many Photos Should You Upload to Google Business Profile to Rank Higher on Maps

There's no hard limit, but a competitive local business should keep at least 20–30 current photos live and add 3–5 fresh ones every week. On Google Maps, photos work as a prominence and activity signal, not an aesthetic one: Google reads a steady stream of recent, relevant images as proof the business is open and active. Quantity plus recency beats a one-time perfect gallery.

By Pulse · 2026-10-24 · 4 min read

Most owners treat photos like decoration: upload 10 nice shots once, done. That's the mistake. On Google Maps, photos are one of the clearest signals of activity and prominence — and prominence is one of the three factors Google uses to decide who wins the local pack. A profile that keeps adding fresh, relevant images tells Google the business is alive. A frozen gallery from 2021 tells it the opposite.

Why photos affect your Google Maps ranking (and how)

Photos are an activity signal, not a beauty contest. Google doesn't rank you because your plates look good — it ranks you partly because you keep feeding the profile. Every upload is a small timestamp that says "this business is operating." Combined with reviews, responses, and posts, photo activity builds the prominence half of Google's relevance–distance–prominence formula.

This also means a competitor who uploads weekly can quietly out-signal you even if your business is better in real life. The gallery is a scoreboard most owners never check.

How many photos — and how often

There's no official cap, but here's a practical cheat-sheet that holds up in big-city markets from Miami to Mexico City to São Paulo:

  • Baseline live: 20–30 current photos minimum for a service business; 30–50+ for restaurants and retail where visuals drive the click.
  • Upload cadence: 3–5 new photos per week beats 50 photos once a year. Recency is the signal.
  • Coverage: exterior/facade, interior, product or plates, team, and "in action" shots. Variety tells Google what you actually are.
  • Restaurants specifically: keep menu items, the dining room, and the storefront current — these are the photos searchers scan before choosing.

The goal isn't a number you hit and forget. It's a weekly ritual so the profile never goes quiet.

The competitive angle nobody checks: your rival's photo feed

Here's the move most owners miss. Open the profile of the business that keeps beating you in the 3-pack and look at two things:

  1. How many photos they have live — total volume.
  2. How recent the newest ones are — the date on the latest uploads.

If they're adding photos every few days and your last upload was three months ago, you've found part of why Google trusts them more in your zone. This isn't about copying their food shots — it's about matching or beating their activity rhythm. Recency of photos is a real input to who looks "live" in the pack.

Technical specs: size, format, and avoiding quality loss

Google compresses uploads, so give it room to work with:

  • Format: JPG or PNG.
  • Resolution: aim for roughly 1200 x 900 px or larger; minimum 720 x 720.
  • File size: between 10 KB and 5 MB.
  • To avoid Google downgrading your photos: upload high-resolution originals, don't re-screenshot or re-export compressed files, and skip heavy filters or text overlays Google may flag.

Geotagging can add context, but it's a minor factor — a consistent address and well-labeled profile matter far more than EXIF data. Don't obsess over it.

Common mistakes that keep your photos buried

  • Uploading once and stopping. The signal decays; a static gallery reads as inactivity.
  • Ignoring customer photos. You can't delete most customer uploads — you can only flag ones that violate policy. The fix is to outpost them with better, more frequent business photos so yours dominate the top.
  • Letting Google pick an ugly cover. Google often auto-selects the top-engagement image, which may be a bad customer shot. You can't fully force the cover, but uploading strong, relevant images consistently shifts what surfaces first.
  • Leaning only on the facade. Interior and "in use" photos usually help more than a single storefront shot because they show searchers what the experience looks like.
  • Multi-location chaos. Each branch posts whatever, whenever — so some locations look active and others look abandoned.

One criterion across every branch

If you run multiple locations, the real risk isn't one weak photo — it's inconsistency. One branch uploads weekly, another hasn't posted since it opened, and Google treats them very differently in their respective zones. A unified standard — same cadence, same photo categories, same quality bar per location — keeps every zone sending the same "we're active" signal.

Frequently asked questions

Do photos affect Google Maps ranking?

Yes, indirectly. Photos are an activity and prominence signal — Google reads frequent, relevant uploads as proof the business is active and operating. They won't outrank distance or core relevance alone, but a steady photo feed strengthens the prominence factor that decides close contests in the local pack.

How many photos should a business have on Google Maps?

Keep at least 20–30 current photos live for a service business, and 30–50 or more for restaurants and retail where visuals drive clicks. There's no official cap, so the real target is coverage across exterior, interior, product, and team shots rather than a single magic number.

How often should I upload new photos?

Aim for 3–5 fresh photos per week. Consistent recency beats a one-time batch — a profile that keeps posting looks active, while a gallery that hasn't changed in months signals the opposite to Google and to searchers.

Can I delete photos that customers upload?

No, you generally can't delete customer photos — you can only flag ones that violate Google's policies. The practical fix is to upload better, more frequent business photos so your images dominate the top of the gallery and push weak customer shots down.

What resolution and format does Google want for photos?

Use JPG or PNG, roughly 1200 x 900 px or larger (720 x 720 minimum), with file sizes between 10 KB and 5 MB. Upload high-resolution originals and avoid re-compressed exports, heavy filters, or text overlays so Google doesn't downgrade or reject them.

Does recent photo activity matter for appearing in the 3-pack?

Yes. Recency is part of the activity signal — a business adding photos every few days looks more live to Google than one with a frozen gallery. Check the competitor beating you in the pack: if their newest photos are days old and yours are months old, that gap is part of the reason.
